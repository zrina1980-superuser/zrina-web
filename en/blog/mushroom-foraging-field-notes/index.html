Mushroom foraging: field notes worth taking home

How to photograph and record a find, preserve your observations and act responsibly around unfamiliar mushrooms.

A walk in the woods can easily leave you with a collection of photos and no context: where was the find, what was its habitat like and what did you notice at the time? A few careful notes can help you remember your observations and follow what you find throughout the season.

A field journal is for learning and recording the experience. A photograph or an app alone cannot confirm that a mushroom is safe to eat.

Photograph more than the cap

If it's safe and you can do so without damaging the habitat, record the whole specimen, the cap, stem and underside. Include a wider view of the surroundings too. Light, angle and a sense of scale can make the photos more useful later.

Note the context

  • Date and approximate location: choose the level of detail that suits you; you don't have to publish sensitive locations.
  • Habitat: describe the surroundings and vegetation you can recognise, without guessing.
  • Conditions: note the weather, moisture or anything else that seemed important.
  • Observations: record colour, shape and visible changes you can compare with your photos.

Leave room for uncertainty

If you're unsure of the name, write “unknown mushroom” or record only your observations. That's more responsible than locking an incorrect identification into your journal. To learn, compare sources and speak with an experienced, trusted person.

Safety matters: Never eat a mushroom based on an app or a photograph. If you don't know it, don't eat it unless a trusted expert has examined the specimen in person and confirmed it, however confident you feel. If any doubt remains, don't eat it.

Technology as a notebook, not a judge

Gljivobook combines a species guide, a morphology filter and a personal journal. Its on-device identifier can help narrow the possibilities using observed features and photos, without a Gemini API key or an internet connection.

With an internet connection and your own Gemini API key, you can also choose an AI scanner that suggests possible species from photos and observations. It can speed up the initial search: then read the descriptions, compare features and possible lookalikes, and record what you observed. An AI suggestion does not confirm the species or its edibility.

When taking photos and making notes, respect nature, local rules and other visitors' privacy. Don't leave litter or damage the habitat for a better photo.
