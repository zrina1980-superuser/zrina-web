Family memories · 11 November 2026

A family recipe is more than a list of ingredients

How to preserve family recipes along with the handwriting, stories and little details that can't be measured with a spoon.

In many families, recipes live on yellowing paper, in a flour-stained notebook or in someone's memory. Alongside the ingredients are the little things that make a recipe ours: who made it, for which occasion, and what “add a little — you know how much” actually means.

Digitising a recipe isn't just about putting measurements in a table. It's about keeping enough context for someone else to make it — and feel a little of the story that goes with it.

1. Keep the original before transcribing it

Photograph the whole page, including its edges, added notes and handwriting. Don't throw away or alter the original as soon as you've made a clean digital copy. The photograph preserves its appearance and signs of use; the transcription makes it easier to read and search.

2. Separate what is written from what you assume

If a word is illegible or a measurement is unclear, mark it as uncertain instead of presenting a guess as the original. You can add your own note — for example, how you interpreted “a cup” — but make it clear that it's an addition.

3. Record the story and practical details

Who did the recipe belong to? Was it made for Christmas, Sunday lunch or family gatherings? Does an ingredient go by a different name today? If you know them, record the order of steps, pan size, oven temperature or the sign that tells you the dish is ready.

4. Let the family version evolve

Recipes change: someone uses less sugar, someone adds a spice, and someone finally discovers how long the dough really needs kneading. Instead of arguing over which version is “right”, keep the original and record changes as new family variations.

5. Share thoughtfully

Before sharing, ask your family what they're happy to make public. Photos of handwritten pages may contain names, addresses or other private notes. A family collection doesn't have to be public to be available to the people it's meant for.

That idea inspired Mamina Teka: a place for a photo of an old recipe, a readable transcription, family variations and the memory behind a dish. Technology doesn't replace the notebook — it helps preserve and find what's inside it.

Mamina Teka with and without AI

You can browse and edit recipes you've already saved and, by signing in with Google, sync and share them with your family. Those steps don't require a Gemini API key.

When you add a recipe from a photo of handwriting, your own Gemini API key lets AI suggest a transcription and organise the details for review. Before saving, compare the text and quantities with the original, especially when the handwriting is unclear.

With a key, you can also try generating an illustration of the dish from the recipe. It's an AI illustration, not a photograph of the actual prepared dish; availability and any cost depend on Google's model, your account and quotas. AI processing requires an internet connection.

Start small: choose one recipe you'd hate to lose. Photograph it, transcribe it without hiding the unclear parts, and add one sentence about the person who made it.
